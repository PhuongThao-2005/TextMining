\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

This section positions L\_RAG at the intersection of retrieval-augmented
generation, legal information retrieval, hybrid ranking, knowledge-graph
retrieval, reranking, and citation-grounded generation. It first establishes
the standard RAG workflow, then identifies the legal properties that ordinary
text retrieval does not capture. The purpose is not to claim that one
paradigm solves legal QA by itself, but to make explicit which prior ideas
motivate each component and which evaluation check tests the corresponding
design assumption.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}
\label{sec:related-rag}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)~\cite{lewis2020retrieval} supplements a
language model's parameters with documents retrieved at query time. A
standard pipeline contains five logical stages: (1) represent the query and
retrieve candidates from an indexed corpus; (2) select, order, and possibly
expand a bounded context; (3) serialize that context with provenance into a
prompt; (4) generate an answer conditioned on the prompt; and (5) check
whether the answer is supported by, and correctly attributes, the supplied
evidence. The external context can reduce dependence on stale parametric
knowledge and gives a user sources to inspect, but it does not guarantee that
the selected passages are sufficient or that the generated claims follow from
them.

The basic RAG abstraction is useful for L\_RAG because it separates three
possible failure locations: the retriever can miss the governing passage, the
context builder can omit its parent or exception, and the generator can fail
to use or cite the evidence. Advanced methods such as Self-RAG~\cite{asai2023self}
and Corrective RAG~\cite{yan2024corrective} add self-reflection or retrieval
correction. They motivate measuring evidence availability and abstention, but
they do not by themselves encode the formal hierarchy, amendment history, or
temporal validity of Vietnamese legal instruments.

\subsection{Legal Information Retrieval}
\label{sec:related-legal-ir}

Legal Information Retrieval (Legal IR) is a specialized area of information
retrieval and legal informatics~\cite{locke2022legal}. Legal texts differ from
open-domain documents in ways that change both indexing and evaluation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical structure.} An instrument is organized into
    parts, chapters, articles, clauses, and items. The meaning of a chunk can
    depend on its parent heading or a neighboring exception.
    \item \textbf{Precision requirements.} A missing qualifier, number, or
    negation can change the interpretation of a rule even when the retrieved
    paragraph is topically correct.
    \item \textbf{Authority and validity.} The Constitution, laws, decrees,
    decisions, and circulars occupy different authority levels, and the
    applicable instrument depends on effective dates and amendment status.
    \item \textbf{Explicit dependencies.} A provision may cite, amend,
    replace, or repeal another instrument. These relationships are evidence
    about context, not ordinary lexical similarity.
\end{enumerate}

Legal-BERT~\cite{chalkidis2020legal_bert} and Lawformer~\cite{xiao2021lawformer}
show how domain-adapted transformers can represent legal language. Such
encoders improve semantic matching, but a vector alone does not state whether
an article is current, whether a reference is authoritative, or which sibling
chunk supplies a missing exception. L\_RAG therefore retains structured
identifiers and graph relations alongside the embedded text.

\subsection{Dense, Sparse, and Hybrid Retrieval}
\label{sec:related-hybrid}

Dense bi-encoders are effective for paraphrase and vocabulary variation,
whereas sparse methods such as BM25~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic} reward
exact term overlap and remain strong for document codes, names, numbers, and
technical terminology. E5~\cite{wang2022e5} motivates the dense component in
L\_RAG; BM25 supplies a lexical complement when a question uses a statutory
phrase verbatim. The two methods fail differently, but complementarity does
not imply that every fusion configuration will improve the final ranking.
Adding a modality can change candidate ordering, tokenization behavior, and
latency, so the trade-off must be established empirically.

Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)~\cite{cormack2009reciprocal} combines ranked
lists without requiring calibrated scores. For a candidate $d$ and retrieval
systems $m \in M$,
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{RRF}(d)=\sum_{m\in M}\frac{1}{k+r_m(d)},
\end{equation}
where $r_m(d)$ is the rank assigned by system $m$ and $k$ is a smoothing
constant. L\_RAG uses $k=60$ in its RRF configurations. Because fusion can
help one query while harming another, the ablation reports paired recall,
rank, and latency deltas rather than assuming that hybrid retrieval is an
unconditional improvement.

\subsection{Knowledge-Graph-Augmented Retrieval}
\label{sec:related-graph}

Knowledge graphs represent entities and explicit relationships as triples
$(e_1,r,e_2)$. GraphRAG~\cite{edge2024graphrag} shows how graph structure can
support query-focused retrieval and multi-hop context. In a legal corpus, a
graph can connect a document to its provisions, a provision to its chunks,
adjacent chunks in reading order, and one instrument to another through
citation, amendment, replacement, or repeal edges.

This structure can restore context that a fixed-size vector chunk omits. It
also introduces failure modes that do not occur in a simple vector index:
incorrect edge direction, stale validity fields, and a traversal policy that
replaces high-confidence seeds under a fixed context budget. L\_RAG's G-LRAG
overlay therefore applies validity and authority filters at query time and
tests one-hop expansion as a distinct ablation. The graph result is
interpreted as evidence about the tested policy, not about graph retrieval in
general.

\subsection{Reranking and Citation-Grounded Generation}
\label{sec:related-reranking}

Cross-encoder rerankers score the query and candidate passage jointly rather
than comparing two independently encoded vectors. Passage reranking with
BERT~\cite{nogueira2019passage} motivates this second-stage design: it can
improve the order of evidence at the cost of additional inference time. L\_RAG
compares multilingual backends including BGE-Reranker-v2-m3~\cite{chen2024bge},
Jina Reranker v2~\cite{jina2024jina}, and Qwen3 Reranker~\cite{qwen2025qwen3}
over a shared 30-candidate pool. Holding the pool fixed makes a positive
effect interpretable as evidence promotion rather than new evidence access.

Citation-grounded generation adds source identifiers or provision references
to the answer format and asks the generator to abstain when context is
insufficient. Work on attributed generation distinguishes whether citations
are present from whether they entail the associated claims and collectively
cover the answer~\cite{gao2023enabling}. These controls improve
inspectability, but a citation marker is not the same as claim-to-source
entailment. The report consequently treats citation presence, structural
coverage, and refusal markers as observable diagnostics and does not convert
them into legal-correctness labels.

L\_RAG operationalizes these requirements through structured corpus records,
the G-LRAG overlay, frozen per-question artifacts, and paired analyses. It also
makes the remaining evidence gaps explicit: the current automatic checks do
not validate temporal applicability, claim-level citation entailment, or the
substantive legal correctness of generated answers. The dataset and benchmark
that make the implemented checks reproducible are described next.
